## 1. The Work

A 45-chapter literary novel (~241,000–245,000 words) about Chicano and Yaqui
soldiers in the Vietnam War and the institutional record that miscounted them.
The structural spine is a dual timeline: 1968 combat narrative and a 2024 Senate
hearing at which the surviving witness testifies. The central argument — the
"counter-algorithm" — is that the same administrative systems that processed the
war's dead also erased Hispanic soldiers from the record, and that family and
community memory (obituaries, parish records, oral testimony) constitute the
counter-archive that corrects it.

The novel is grounded in the author's family history: Yaqui lineage through his
grandmother Nela and great-grandmother Avina Sagasta, displaced from San Javier,
Sonora, 1912–1915.

**Field position.** There is no long-form Chicano/Yaqui Vietnam War novel at this
ambition level in the American canon; the existing field is oral history
(Trujillo's *Soldados*) and anthology (Mariscal's *Aztlán and Viet Nam*). The
closest structural peer is Viet Thanh Nguyen's *The Sympathizer*. This
positioning is the author's and his external reviewers' assessment — testing it
is one of the questions posed to reviewers below (§5).

## 2. What Is Verified (and how verification is governed)

The project runs a formal adjudication discipline: every factual claim carries a
status tag (CONFIRMED / PROBABLE / PARTIALLY VERIFIED / UNVERIFIED), sources are
encoded in Library of Congress BIBFRAME 2.0 before being treated as settled, and
fictional statistics are labeled as fictional.

**Settled primary record (CONFIRMED):**
- Total U.S. Vietnam casualties: 58,220 (NARA Defense Casualty Analysis System, identifier 2163536).
- Hispanic-coded casualties in DCAS: 349 (0.599%) — treated in this project as an *institutional undercount artifact*, never as a valid estimate.
- Independent estimate of Hispanic casualties: **2,309–3,741** (surname-based BISG methodology, five-stream convergence, median ≈ 3,070). Reported only as a range, by rule.
- Deported U.S. veterans population: 3,100+ (GAO, ACLU); ICE veteran-tracking gap 98%+ (GAO-19-416); IIRIRA 1996 retroactive aggravated-felony expansion (8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(43)).
- Historical anchors used in the narrative and their public record: Roy Benavidez (Yaqui-Mexican MOH recipient, 13-year "insufficient documentation" delay, 1983 SSA fight); Everett Alvarez Jr. (first American POW, Mexican American, shot down Aug 5, 1964, held 8 years 7 months); Operation Babylift (April 1975, C-5A crash, "not orphans" identity-erasure controversy); IBM System/360 (announced April 7, 1964) as the era's administrative computing platform.

**Scholarly base:** ~20 peer-reviewed and dissertation sources, anchored by
Guzman's casualty methodology, Jimenez (2014) "The Missing American," Mariscal,
Oropeza, Gartner & Segura (2000), and Zhang & Lee (2025, *APSR*).

## 3. What Is Open (the honest register)

The author's position is that these are disclosed to reviewers, not smoothed over.

**A. Integrity remediation (highest priority, in progress).**
During restoration after a data breach, one chapter (Ch. 42, generational claims)
was found to contain an AI-generated transaction ledger from a prior tool
session — fabricated document IDs presented as archival evidence. It was
detected by the project's own oversight process, quarantined, and will be
retired and replaced with verifiable primary sources. The identified archival
paths: NARA RG 84, AGN Fondo Gobernación (Mexico), Archivo Histórico del Estado
de Sonora, Sonoran parish sacramental records, Wells Fargo Corporate Archives,
and Catholic diocesan press archives. **The manuscript will not be submitted
anywhere until this is complete.** The author treats this episode as a
demonstration of why the project's verification governance exists.

**B. Continuity items under adjudication.**
A small set of continuity conflicts produced by multi-platform drafting are
logged and awaiting the author's ruling: the canonical Chapter 1 closing line
(two locked variants exist in the record); one measured chapter-pair overlap
requiring de-duplication; a character-death chapter discrepancy between the
character bible and the manuscript text (the text is authoritative); a name
spelling standardization (Linh/Lihn); several chapters below the project's
word-count band pending an expand-or-hold decision.

**C. Evidentiary items where the record is genuinely incomplete.**
- Three FOIA requests (VA BIRLS, DHS ENFORCE, DOD Archives) are past statutory windows; constructive-exhaustion litigation posture is available.
- The BISG surname analysis should be re-run against the full Census surname file for peer-review defensibility.
- The percentage form of the casualty-disparity claim used in the text must be re-derived from the settled range before submission.

**D. Cultural governance gate.**
The novel contains Yaqui ceremony content. Project rule: **tribal consultation
before publication** — research is not permission. Contact path identified
(Pascua Yaqui Tribe cultural office). No ceremony detail is extrapolated beyond
what the author's own family transmission and the published ethnographic record
support. This gate is treated as equal in weight to the evidentiary gates.

## 4. How the Manuscript Is Evaluated

Three instruments are used internally (a 150-point literary rubric for
submission readiness; a regression-based prose-craft score; a positioning index
mapped to prize-jury citation language). External reviewers have also applied
their own frameworks. The instruments disagree in places — the author's view is
that instrument disagreement is information, not noise, and the current
priority is locking a single certification target before any score is cited
externally. **For peer review purposes: the numbers are internal process tools.
The questions in §5 are what the author actually wants judged.**

What the internal record consistently shows: the strongest chapters (the opening
Yaqui ceremony; the My Lai chapter; the Senate-hearing latrine intercut; the
"Last Dance" action chapter) are the manuscript's ceiling and are stable across
every instrument; the open work is concentrated in continuity, evidentiary
hygiene, and a handful of under-length chapters — not in the prose itself.

## 5. Questions for Mentors and Peer Reviewers

1. **The bright line between fiction and evidence.** The novel embeds real casualty data and real institutional history inside a fictional narrative. Does the current architecture keep the line legible to a reader — and to a hostile reviewer? Where does it blur?
2. **The counter-algorithm thesis as history.** Is the claim — administrative classification systems produced demographic erasure of Hispanic casualties — supportable at the strength the novel implies, given the DCAS record and the BISG range? Where would a historian push back? (Note: the project deliberately avoids implying corporate intentionality the evidence doesn't support.)
3. **The BISG methodology.** Is surname-based estimation against the 349-count DCAS baseline defensible for public-facing claims once re-run on the full Census file? What would a demographer require?
4. **Yaqui representation.** Beyond the consultation gate: is the ceremony material's framing (ancestral presence rendered as fact within the narrative, analytical register maintained) respectful and defensible? What review process would you recommend before publication?
5. **Field position.** Does the comparison set (*The Sympathizer*, *Matterhorn*, *The Things They Carried*, *Bless Me, Ultima*) hold? What is missing from the comp list?
6. **Peer-review pathway.** Given the dual nature of the project (literary manuscript + public-policy evidentiary work under the AUMER Foundation), what venue sequence would you advise — literary submission first, or the scholarly/archival apparatus first so the novel stands on a reviewed evidentiary base?
